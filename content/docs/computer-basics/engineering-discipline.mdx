## Lesson 01 — A wish is not a plan

> "You never gave the AI a plan. You gave it a wish."

### The problem

You open the agent and type what you want. It builds fast — front end, back end, database. It feels great. Then you look closer: it's missing things you wanted and added things you never asked for. So you prompt again. You fix one thing and something else quietly breaks. A few prompts in, you've half forgotten what you originally set out to build, and you're going in circles re-explaining, re-fixing, burning hours and tokens just to get back to something you thought you already had.

### Why it happens

No serious engineering team opens the editor first. They answer: *What are we building? What's in version one? What's explicitly out? In what order?* Requirements before code — the oldest discipline in software. It exists for one boring reason:

- Changing a line in a plan is **free**.
- Changing a decision already spread across your codebase is a **rewrite**.

When we started building with AI, we quietly threw that step out and jumped straight to "just build it."

### The practice `scope`

Instead of jumping into code, do what a senior engineer does first — interview:

1. Who is this for?
2. What's in version one, and what waits?
3. What depends on what?
4. Then choose how to shape the build:
   - Prove one thin slice through the whole system first, or
   - Ship the smallest usable version, or
   - Finish one full journey at a time.
5. Get a recommendation — and the reason for it — then choose.

> **Keep tech out of the plan on purpose:** The plan decides *what* you're building. The stack, database, and libraries are a separate decision that comes right after — so your plan doesn't rot the day you change a tool.

> **Takeaway:** Turn a vague idea into a real, ordered plan that *you* made — not one the AI guessed in a hurry.

## Lesson 02 — Decisions must be made on purpose

> "You don't decide. You inherit those decisions — and you inherit the breaks that come with them."

### The problem

A plan isn't a design. You still have to decide how the system is actually built: how the database is designed, what stack you choose, how critical logic is handled, how the thing is architected so it holds up where it's going. On day one you won't have millions of users — but you have to build in a way that could get there without overbuilding for a scale you don't have yet.

That balance is a real engineering decision, and it's exactly what goes wrong when you let the AI make it for you. It does make it for you:

- How the data is stored
- What happens on failure
- Whether that list even paginates
- Which provider you're using

The AI doesn't stop and ask any of it. It picks whatever makes it run, and buries your choice in the code.

### The practice `architect`

Run the design conversation a senior engineer would run — before any code.

- **Think in patterns first.** Does this even need to be complicated, or is the boring, reliable choice the right one?
- **Come in with opinions.** Start with a monolith. Use a relational database by default. Paginate every list. Rate limit every public endpoint. Never keep secrets in your code.
- **Put options on the table.** For every real decision: the recommendation, the honest alternative, and the actual reason it lost. Written down where you can see it and overrule it — not buried in code.

> **The safeguard:** for every single value the feature has to show or compute — a total, a date, a status — name where that value comes from. Anything without a real source is a decision nobody made. Catch it at design time instead of letting the AI invent it later, mid-build.

> **Takeaway:** Every feature has decisions hiding inside it that you never specified. Make them visible, deliberate, and reversible.

## Lesson 03 — Refuse to build on unmade decisions

> "An AI that turns down work. That's a genuinely different way to build."

### The problem

An AI will happily convince itself that a real decision is "just wiring" and push right past it. Feelings are not a reliable detector for invented decisions.

### The practice `develop`

Don't detect it by feeling. Detect it mechanically:

1. List every value the feature has to produce.
2. Check the plan and say where each one comes from.
3. Any value with no source is **a decision that is owed**.
4. Stop there and send the decision back to be made.

> **You can still override it:** You're always in charge. But the moment you do, the assumption gets written down and flagged on that feature until it's properly decided — so even the corner you cut is visible, sitting in a file instead of being lost in the chat forever.

### The division of labor

| The AI's job | Your job |
| --- | --- |
| Silently inventing a storage strategy, a failure mode, a pagination rule | Making the call, on purpose, with the trade-offs visible |
| Building on top of an assumption nobody chose | Deciding whether to accept it, change it, or defer it |

> **Be honest about the limits:** no specific prompt catches 100% of these. But catching almost all of them — and giving you a senior engineer's judgment on the rest — is a completely different world from making all of them silently.

> **Takeaway:** You are the decision maker. The workflow's job is to make sure nothing important gets decided behind your back.

## Lesson 04 — State belongs in files, not in chat

> "Your project's knowledge no longer lives in a chat that vanishes when you close it. It lives in a file."

### The problem

Every agentic setup runs on a context file — `AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, whatever your tool calls it. It's the file that tells the agent how your project actually works: the stack, the commands, the conventions.

Without it:

- Every new session opens blind and just guesses.
- It guesses differently every time.
- Your codebase quietly drifts into three different styles.

And setting one up well is more work than it sounds — especially once your app isn't one simple project. In a monorepo or a service split, one giant file bloats every session with rules it doesn't need.

### The practice `audit`

Read the actual project — its structure, its stack, the decisions already made — and write the context from what's really there, not from what the AI assumes a project like yours would look like.

- **Single app:** one clean agent file.
- **Monorepo:** a lean root file for what's true everywhere, plus separate files next to the parts with their own rules.
- **On a real team's repo:** never overwrite what a person wrote. Handwritten edits stay. Only the gaps get filled.
- **On conflict:** when the code disproves something the docs claim, flag it and let a human decide — don't bulldoze it.

### Keep it true `sync`

As you keep shipping, reconcile the context files against what the repo actually shows now — so the context you read in month three still describes the app you actually have.

> **Takeaway:** A fresh session, a teammate, or a completely different AI tool reads the files and picks up where you left off. Nobody re-explains the project to the AI ever again.

## Lesson 05 — The real job is inherited code

> "That's not your typical Tuesday evening. Your Tuesday codebase is a codebase that somebody else wrote, most often years old."

### The problem

Most AI coding tutorials — this one included — build on a brand new project. It gives you a clean slate, nothing to fight, easy to learn from. But the real job looks different:

- Somebody else wrote it, often years ago.
- No documentation, or a very outdated one.
- Conventions you have to reverse-engineer to even figure out.
- Half the time it's not even the stack the tutorials use.

The real question isn't "can AI read this?" Of course it can. The question is: **can a whole engineering workflow actually operate on a mess you inherited?**

### The practice `audit`

Point the same audit skill at a real project. Instead of setting up something new, it reads something real — whatever it is — and writes down how it actually works. It doesn't care that it isn't JavaScript. Pointed at a 15-year-old PHP codebase with no JavaScript anywhere in sight, it reads and documents it just the same.

> **Because what it's capturing is engineering context, not a framework.**

### Then the workflow bends around reality

- **`scope`** doesn't pretend the project starts today. It enrolls what's already been built, then plans your next slice on top of reality.
- **`architect`** designs new features against the constraints the existing system already imposes — the data it has, the patterns it follows — not in a vacuum.

The workflow works *with* the code that's there instead of fighting it. That's really the whole point: **reusing instead of regenerating.**

> **Takeaway:** Plenty of tools can glance at a legacy codebase. The point is that the entire discipline — plan, decide, build — runs on top of code you didn't write, in whatever language it happens to be in.

## Lesson 06 — "It works" is not done

> "Green tests only prove the code that the AI thought to test. They never prove the feature actually exists."

### The problem

The AI says done. It works. And it doesn't. The button doesn't do anything. The edge case crashes. Or the thing the plan asked for was never actually built. And the trap that makes it worse: the tests are green.

### The practice — four jobs, kept separate on purpose

| Job | What it actually does |
| --- | --- |
| verify | Runs the *real app* — not reads the codebase and nods. Drives the actual feature, clicks the flow, hits the endpoint, watches what happens, and checks it against the criteria the plan set out. Every screen the plan promised, actually built. |
| test | Writes the test suite a senior engineer would write: tests for what a caller actually relies on and what would genuinely break something. That's what protects this feature six months from now. |
| check / review | Reads the code on a *different model* than the one that wrote it, because a model reviewing its own work carries its own blind spots. A fresh set of eyes reads the diff and ranks what it finds. |
| document | Writes the human record — the PR description, the changelog — from the actual diff, not from the AI's memory of what it thinks it changed. |

> **Match the effort to the risk:** How much of this you run is up to you and the project. A throwaway prototype just self-checks. A payment system runs all four.

> **Why this gets skipped:** it isn't flashy. It's also the part that decides whether the feature you just added actually works — and didn't quietly break the three before it.

> **Takeaway:** The difference between "the tests pass" and "I watched it work with my own eyes."

## Lesson 07 — Debug with discipline, not vibes

> "20 minutes later, you've got five random edits, the original bug, and two brand new ones."

### The problem

Something's broken. You tell the AI to fix it and it starts thrashing. Changes one thing — still broken. Changes three more — still broken. That's a pattern-matching "try a fix" instead of finding the right cause. That's the decay in its purest form: trying to fix one thing and making three more.

### The practice `debug`

1. **Reproduce reliably first.** A bug you can't reproduce on command is a bug you can't prove you fixed.
2. **Narrow to the smallest failing spot.**
3. **Form just one theory** about the root cause and test that one thing before touching anything else.
4. **If the theory is wrong, throw the change away.** Learn from it. Don't leave a dead edit lying around making things messier.
5. **Fix the cause, not the symptom.** Don't clamp a null to make the error disappear — find out why that value was null in the first place.
6. **Write a test that fails without the fix and passes with it**, so that exact bug can never quietly come back.
7. **Go hunting.** Look for the same mistake hiding somewhere else in the code.
8. **Know when it isn't a coding bug.** If it turns out the bug is a bad decision, say so and send it back to be redesigned properly — instead of papering over a bad call with a patch.

> **The discipline that matters:** do one thing at a time, where the result actually means something.

> **Takeaway:** An AI that reacts to a broken symptom versus one that investigates a cause.

## Lesson 08 — Match the workflow to the project

> "It guides you the way a senior engineer would, instead of forcing every project into the same process."

### The problem

A rigid process is its own failure mode. A small app doesn't need the same workflow as a massive codebase. Working on a brand new project is very different from working on something that's been around for years.

### The practice

The workflow looks at what you're actually building, how big it is, and where it's going — and adapts:

- Small, low-risk, throwaway → light touch. Self-check.
- Large, long-lived, high-risk → full pipeline. All the verification jobs.
- Greenfield → plan and design from first principles.
- Inherited → audit first, then plan on top of what exists.

> **Takeaway:** That's the difference between having a bunch of prompts and having an actual engineering system.

## Lesson 09 — Tools are not judgment

> "You can't install judgment from a repo."

### The problem

Running the skills is the easy part. A week in, you'll run straight into the honest thing:

- Knowing *when* to run which one.
- What to decide when it puts a choice in front of you.
- Whether the AI's recommendation is actually right for your situation.
- What to do when the plan is wrong, or a session goes sideways.

That's the judgment. And it's not in the repo.

> **The tools are one piece:** The judgment is everything around them: how these agents actually work under the hood, how to run all of this as one system instead of a pile of separate commands, how to build your own skills, how to take it into a real legacy codebase, and what to do when it all goes wrong — which is, honestly, where the real engineering lives, and where every free demo quietly stops.

> **Takeaway:** The repo gives you the tools. The mindset, the skills, and the judgment behind them are yours to build.

## The whole thing

Six moves that make a project compound instead of decay.

1. **Plan it properly** — Requirements before code. Decide what to build first, and in what order.
2. **Make the decisions on purpose** — Nothing important gets chosen silently inside a feature you didn't specify.
3. **Keep the state in files** — The project's knowledge outlives the chat, the session, and the tool.
4. **Work on real code** — Inherited, undocumented, unfamiliar languages. That's the actual job.
5. **Prove it actually runs** — Green tests are not evidence. Watch the feature work against the plan.
6. **Fix it with discipline when it breaks** — Reproduce, isolate, one theory at a time, fix the cause, lock it with a test.

> **Take this with you either way:** The difference between someone who prompts an AI and someone who engineers with it was never a better model or a smarter prompt. It's this: decide what to build first. Make the hard calls on purpose. Keep the state in files. And never let the AI decide something important without telling you.
