# 15 Years Building Software

If you get one tip from the talk "If I Were Starting Today", it is this: disruption moves fast, adaptation is slow. You cannot control the first. You can train the second.

This file merges three variants of the same talk into one plain-markdown guide. The base order is 19 lessons in 5 parts. Numbers preserved from source: 15 yrs building, 6 months daily agents ~= 2 years solo study, ~4hr React course, $500 -> $1500 -> $28k, $180k base / $240k total video offer.

## Part I — The framing

### 1. Disruption moves fast, adaptation is slow

> "If you get nothing from this video except this one tip, you're going to be much better off."

This is the frame for everything else. When you are early on, you might be on track. But if you are not keeping up with trends, new capabilities, and new approaches to work, you slowly diverge from the proper path.

This is an extremely common problem for people deeper in their career. "I have 10 years of experience. 15 years. 30 years." But it is not the experience that actually matters today.

> **Warning:** Why a senior engineer can lose to an entry-level engineer: if the entry-level engineer focused the majority of their effort on the thing that is disrupting the industry, they have the skill the role is actually hiring for. You may have a higher total skill level. They have the right skill.

#### What to do about it

- If you are very experienced: spend a little extra deliberate time to keep up with anything new. You do not need to abandon 15 years, just close the gap.
- If you are brand new: use it as an advantage by focusing your effort on what is currently in demand. You can point 100% of a smaller effort straight at it.
- Treat "on track" as a decaying state. Schedule weekly trend scans: tools, capabilities, workflows — especially things that threaten your stack.
- Measure adaptation speed, not years. Ask monthly: "What can I do now that I could not do 90 days ago?"

> **Takeaway:** You can't control how fast the industry moves. You can control how fast you adapt — and that's the whole game.

### 2. 30 years of the wrong experience

> "It's 30 years of the incorrect experience, or just the same experience over and over again."

The trap: "I have 30 years of experience." But that can quietly mean 30 years of the incorrect experience — or worse, the same one year repeated 30 times.

#### What to do about it

- Focus on what is valuable right now.
- Focus on what companies are looking for.
- Focus on what you need to be relevant in the industry.
- Do not get stuck on "we've always done it this way, so this is the correct way."

> **Warning:** Innovation is very quick, things adapt fast, but people and systems lag behind. So what's taught in university right now is probably not what you need to know right now to get a job.

> **Takeaway:** Years are not a credential. Relevant years are.

### 3. Don't split fundamentals from AI

> "You're asking me what I would do if I was in your situation, and my answer is that I would focus on the fundamentals and AI-enhanced development and learning."

Someone new emails: "What can I do to learn as fast as I possibly can, and get as good as I possibly can?"

He answers: "Utilize AI agents to help enhance your progress."

And they say: "Whoa, excuse me — I don't want to be using AI because that's going to slow down my learning. I've just been doing everything solo, learning the old school patterns of work."

> **Warning:** The problem isn't AI. It's the approach. The belief that using AI is somehow going to destroy your foundation. That's a belief about how you use it, not a fact about the tool.

The three modes of using AI:

1. You write the code — no agent in the loop. You type the loops, you fight the syntax, you hit the bugs. Slowest mode, and the only one that builds muscle memory. Use when learning a fundamental or logic is critical. The pain is the point — it builds the intuition that tells you when AI output is subtly wrong.
2. Agent writes, you direct — you describe interface, behavior, constraints. Agent produces implementation. Use when building something real and design is settled. Failure mode is "vibe coder" — accepting output without understanding it. You avoid that by doing mode 1 first.
3. Agent teaches, you write — agent does not touch your code. It explains, quizzes you, generates examples, gives you tests to pass, reviews your notes for flaws, shows the space of possible approaches. This is how you go from "the AI wrote it and I don't know why" to actually understanding.

Why "I won't touch AI until I'm senior" makes zero sense:

- You force yourself into the structure of people 20+ years in who are failing to adapt.
- You adopt their bottleneck before you have even started.

#### What to do — both

- Yes, know how to code.
- Absolutely, understand all the fundamentals — start there.
- Pair it with AI learning and use of agents for system design, architecture, bugs, and security.

> **Tip:** His own data point: using agents pretty much every day for the last 6 months, the amount of learning he's gone through is comparable to probably a couple of years studying on his own.

> **Takeaway:** It'll make your software better, not worse. Don't split these ideas — do both.

## Part II — How to learn

### 4. Find the person. Collapse time.

> "I literally would try to find somebody who has more experience than me in whatever it is I'm trying to accomplish on day one."

When he started, he was on his own — wasting time on YouTube, searching the internet. There was no AI back then. Today he uses AI to guide learning, but for anything important — architectural decisions especially — he checks with people more experienced than him.

> **Insight:** Because there's something AI and solo practice cannot replace: the experience that takes years and years to develop.

The mechanism: withdraw from the skills of others. If you are trying to do something beyond your skill set — which is important for learning — the best way is to draw on the skills of others.

#### What to do

- Find someone 2–20 years ahead of you on day one, not someday. Goal is to collapse time.
- Check important architectural decisions against experienced people. AI can sanity-check; humans can say "I've watched that burn down."
- Go network on LinkedIn, Reddit, social media, your existing network. Peer-to-peer communication is still the biggest value for skill, network, and opportunities.
- When someone makes you rewrite it, ask for the why. The rewrite is the fee; the reasoning is the product.
- Low-friction habit he uses: read a book, then message the author on LinkedIn: "Hey, I read your book. Good stuff." A few authors of popular books have responded — yes, some were romance novelists with nothing to do with code — but the mechanism works.

Story — the pair-programming rewrite: at his first job, he pair-programmed with a guy way better than him. The guy irritated him — every time he tried to do something, "No, rewrite it from scratch. You need to do it this way." He thought: "Dude, I just want to get this app built. Every time I talk to you, you're making me redo stuff." Looking back, that was the most helpful time in his journey — because he actually learned why they were doing certain things.

> **Note:** An uncomfortable truth: "I know for some of you it stinks to hear that because you can't just stay behind your computer and just code." But getting experience from other people is going to help a ton.

> **Takeaway:** You can't reproduce 30 years of experience on your own in a weekend. Go borrow it.

### 5. Coins vs the treasure chest

> "They're too busy distracted by this to go open the treasure chest."

The treasure chest was there the whole time. Two hours with someone experienced beats two hundred hours of tutorial hopping — same total "content," wildly different value.

Tutorial hopping gives you little coins. You watch a resource, ask an AI tool how to do something, and you stack up little skills over time. That's what you get. "However, this is going to take you forever to become a millionaire in terms of skill set."

There are much higher-value opportunities people just ignore. They pick up coins, too distracted, and never open the chest.

> **Note:** The honest accounting: back in his early career he spent countless hours watching stuff on YouTube and learned the same amount in terms of value from a couple of things said by someone with more experience. Less total content. Less total findings. Far more value.

#### What to do

- Swap tutorial hours for applied-experience content: engineering blogs, practitioner podcasts — the "here's what happened when…" genre.
- Remember: "The basic tutorial I believe is coming to an end." Most of what we'd look up on YouTube, we can ask an LLM to explain with examples.
- Chase the why-behind-the-choice, not another how-to.

Stories — where the real value lived:

- Zero Knowledge podcast (cryptography): every guest was actually doing cryptography. He learned how they thought through things, their discoveries, their problems — and what wasn't being talked about. That filtered what mattered fast.
- Discord's engineering article on their database structure and NoSQL approach: he could see why they actually needed this database. That "why choose one approach over another" is the true skill, and very hard to learn alone as a beginner.

> **Warning:** Most tutorial content is now commoditized. LLMs explain basics fine. Applied experience is where value moves.

> **Insight:** The real skill: there are all these options and approaches — why would you choose one over the other? That's the true skill, and it's very hard to learn on your own as a beginner.

> **Takeaway:** Applied experience from someone who has been there is not reproducible by AI. It's talking about specific things ChatGPT has never experienced.

### 6. Learn experience AND adapt fast

> "These are kind of like two sides of the same coin."

This seems almost contradictory to lesson 2 — don't get stuck on "we've always done it this way." But they're not in conflict. They're two points of focus working toward the same goal.

The two halves:

- Learn from experience: the 30 years you can't reproduce. Draw on people who've been through it. Learn why things are done. Understand real-world consequences.
- Learn what's new: trends, optimizations, workflows. Keep up with new capabilities so you can be even better than people who came before.

#### What to do

- Learn from experienced people first.
- Then learn what's new and trending to find optimizations and workflows.
- Don't treat these as conflicting ideas — treat them as a single mindset.

> **Takeaway:** Borrow the judgment of the past, and apply the tools of the present.

### 7. General learning + specialization

> "We want to cover a large basis of stuff to get a good broad understanding. And then we build on top of this our skyscraper."

There's still unlimited things to focus on — which is why roadmap videos do so well. People need the bigger picture. But there are two layers:

- Layer 1 — General: broad, wide foundation covering many topics.
- Layer 2 — Specialization: very good at very few things. "Broad" here means something like back-end web development — not a super-specific niche that takes 20 years.

> **Note:** "And I lied. This is actually a top hat." The broad base plus a focused top — not a thin tower on a thin base.

If you spread yourself thin becoming good at a bunch of different things, you're effectively becoming average at a bunch of stuff and great at nothing. That's fine for the foundational layer — but it limits you when providing value others cannot. "You're not good enough at anything to do anything special."

Two problems people hit: they don't know what to specialize in, and they topic-hop, never becoming good at anything.

#### What to do

- Build the brim with non-overlapping fundamentals: networking, databases, back-end development, desktop applications, and low-level programming. A little time in each buys ~80% of the benefit.
- Follow end-to-end flows — point A to point B — don't get distracted. That's why he's a fan of courses: one lesson after another, each building on the last.
- When learning a new topic, find a crash course and watch it end to end.

Story — the ~4hr React course: he watched a ~4-hour React course in a single sitting. Did he know how to build everything in React after? No. But he had practice going through lessons, and a video to anchor back to. As a beginner, it worked — because he could go deeper, stacking knowledge on what he'd just learned.

> **Tip:** Courses are underrated for the foundation layer because they force an end-to-end path instead of scattered research.

> **Takeaway:** A wide base makes you capable. A deep top makes you valuable.

### 8. Go deep instead of hopping

> "I didn't challenge myself. It felt like learning, but it wasn't really learning because it wasn't painful enough."

What he actually did when starting: one day studying jQuery. The next, Angular. Then Apps Script automations — something totally off the wall. Basically whatever he felt like that day. The efficiency was trash. He wasted a lot of time. He'd reach stuff he already knew from previous languages — and go no deeper. Skills stayed flat.

What "going deep" actually looks like: starting at loops, then functions, then generators, then object-oriented programming, then web development, then how async code works — and becoming better as you go deeper. Where he got stuck: somewhere around web development — then switched language and tried again. Flat.

> **Warning:** "Learning is actually painful." If you're learning and think "oh, this is hard, I don't really understand" — that's a good thing, unless it's way too difficult.

> **Note:** And the inverse: if you walk away thinking "that was super easy" without any concrete things you learned, it feels like learning, but you're really just exercising your brain on stuff you already know.

For him, deep learning has always been painful: a lot of focus, cross-referencing, note-taking. He also has a passive mode — podcasts, entertainment, experience over time — but deep learning is more painful than fun.

#### What to do

- Push past the "I already know this" layer. Depth is where undiscovered things live.
- Expect discomfort. Heavy focus, cross-referencing, note-taking is the feeling of new territory.
- Stay on one thing until it's uncomfortable before switching.

> **Insight:** Message to companies and recruiters: if you find a developer who's really good but hasn't worked with your very specific software that nobody cares about — that doesn't matter. If they're a good software developer, they can pick it up.

> **Takeaway:** You want to go deep with something so you become better in general — and that general quality translates to other things much more easily.

### 9. Learn the diff, not the duplicate

> "You don't need to know everything twice. You only need to know something once, and then to learn the second thing it's just a diff between these two."

The trap: you learn loops in Python. Then you go to TypeScript and learn loops there. Little syntax difference. You spend time. It feels productive. But you didn't learn anything new. You just learned how to translate it.

> **Warning:** This is a low-value skill. You should be able to pick it up as needed — by reading the original implementation, reading docs, or using AI tools to translate.

Instead, learn how the two languages differ. This encapsulates the skill of translation.

#### What to do

- Learn object-oriented programming in Python. Then learn how OOP works in JavaScript. That's the diff.
- Note trait/interface languages, functional-first vs class-based, multiple inheritance vs none — those are the interesting differences.
- Pick one language to start — JavaScript or TypeScript, Python, anything — and learn everything you can, not getting distracted.

Story — C to Java translation: he had a Java class and had never used Java — but he had used C, which is basically the same thing from two different companies (overgeneralization, but close). So he wrote code in C and then "Javaified" it, making very minor translations. Translation cost minutes, not weeks.

> **Note:** And it applies to programming languages generally: "I don't really care what you learn. JavaScript or TypeScript. Python. Anything else. Just pick one to start with and learn everything you can, not getting distracted."

> **Takeaway:** One deep implementation plus a diff beats two shallow implementations. Every time.

## Part III — AI as a learning multiplier

### 10. Pick one AI tool and go deep

> "Yeah, I'm an AI power user — which, by the way, I've never written a Claude Code skill or learned how to create my own sub-agents. Like, basic stuff."

Same failure as lesson 9, now in a new domain: some experience with ChatGPT or Codex, some with Claude / Claude Code, maybe Cursor, Antigravity — broad across many platforms, deep in none. So you call yourself a power user without ever having written a skill, created a sub-agent, or done basic deeper work.

#### What to do

- Pick one — he picked Claude Code — and get really good at that.
- Use the forcing function: "I'm going to build an app. Everything I can do with AI, I'm going to figure out how to do it — but not in a sloppy way, how to produce high-quality results."
- Continually go deeper with agentic AI, LLMs, and the tools.
- Why this works: it translates to other tools very easily, because all frameworks have similar patterns. You just translate and get the diff.

> **Takeaway:** Breadth across AI tools is not a skill. Depth in one is — and it ports to the rest.

### 11. Build a knowledge vault

> "Basically going to be reference for you and the AI."

The very first thing he'd do — for studying or for a project — is create a knowledge vault.

#### What to do

- Make a folder full of markdown files.
- If it's a project: all information about that project goes in.
- If studying: your notes go in.
- Have AI help you structure your thoughts.
- Have it correct anything incorrect.
- Result: you organize and enhance workflow faster.

> **Tip:** It serves double duty. It's reference for you and for the AI. That's the point.

> **Takeaway:** Notes you write once, structure with AI, and both of you can read forever.

### 12. Write your own skills

> "What is a skill? It's basically just a prompt you give an LLM, but you can reuse it at any point."

You describe what you want the AI to do, then invoke it like a function.

#### What to do

- Build a Research skill — reusable research prompt.
- Build a Code-translations skill — how might this look in other languages? Specifically, how are they different?
- Build a Design-alternatives skill — the one he calls most valuable here.

Why alternatives is most valuable: you craft it to find different approaches in design, or different patterns people used to teach the same thing — conquering the same topic from different angles. Biggest challenge in modern learning is siloed concepts — not understanding how something translates to real world and similar things.

OOP example: if studying object-oriented programming, you might not know that some languages are functionally organized (functional languages or patterns replacing OOP), some are trait-based (no formal classes, types adhering to traits/interfaces), some have multiple inheritance while others don't. You think "this is the way it's always done" — when others do it completely differently. Have the skill challenge the whole category: do we even need OOP? Are there other architectures even in a language that has OOP? Java forces it; Python doesn't require strict OOP.

> **Warning:** Because the biggest challenge in modern learning is siloed concepts — not understanding how something translates to the real world and to other things that are similar to it.

> **Insight:** Have the skill challenge the whole category. Do we even need object-oriented programming? Are there other architecture types we could use even in a language that has OOP?

> **Takeaway:** These enhancements would set you apart from other beginners far more than refusing to use AI at all — because those people are failing to see so much of the bigger picture.

### 13. Make AI prove things, not just state them

> "Stop thinking of AI as the enemy to learning and instead think of it as an enhancement that can help you learn more faster."

Flaw he sees in his own learning: "I'll read something and I'll assume it's fact because it's stated as fact." It's why his videos showcase examples that prove the fact, rather than just stating it. Rounding, for instance — he'll show an example.

Have AI generate examples for things typically glossed over. Reading docs that say "this is what happens on an overflow" — you could trust and move on. Or have AI generate a quick runnable integer-overflow example you can actually run and see. Running an example embeds it deeper. Before, trusting was faster — but it didn't go as deep.

With AI you can ask:

- Real-world examples of when you'd choose each approach.
- Alternative approaches you haven't considered.
- Code samples that actually produce each behavior.
- Should we use light threads managed by the runtime? OS-managed threads? async/await? Pros and cons of each.

Concurrency example — three tasks, three architectures:

```text
Serial:            T1 -> T2 -> T3 -> "done"
Parallel (wait):   T1 + T2 + T3 run, block until all finish -> "done"
Fire-and-forget:   launch T1, T2, T3, continue immediately, "done" arrives when last finishes
```

Ask AI for runnable code for each plus when each is the right call, and trade-offs of runtime light-threads vs OS threads vs async/await. That turns one pattern into an understood decision space.

He does this in app development too: building a front end, he doesn't do one thing at a time. He fans out a bunch of different agents to give different examples, to see outside current scope. More randomness, new ideas.

Other ways to use AI for learning:

- Give examples of actual implementation.
- Quiz you with questions and answers.
- Write software tests you must implement code to pass.
- Review your notes and find flaws in understanding.

> **Tip:** Running an example embeds it deeper. Before, it was faster to just trust and move on — but it doesn't go as deep in your mind.

> **Note:** The point: there are a million ways to do this kind of thing and it can be overwhelming. AI shows you the possible ways, the trade-offs, and not just one isolated solution.

> **Takeaway:** The isolated solution is what tutorials give you. The space of possible solutions is what actually builds judgment.

## Part IV — Build real things

### 14. Build something real, and keep building it

> "Don't let these projects be basic things that are totally useless."

Start building stuff as soon as possible — not just through work, but your own personal projects.

What not to do: maybe start with a calculator or to-do list to get familiar — fine, to start. But then build something actually cool, taking time, effort, thought. And keep working on the same apps over time.

> **Warning:** His biggest downfall when starting: he'd tinker with something for a day, get bored or not know what to do with it, and move on. It took a long time to start building anything meaningful.

#### What to do

- Pick a medium-difficulty project. Challenging every day, not crushing. He'd now choose easier than crypto-in-Rust.
- Stay on ONE project over time. Tinker-abandon-repeat is the killer; continuity is where depth compounds.
- Figure out every detail, one thing at a time.

Story — Currycoin, the Rust cryptocurrency: he decided "I'm just going to build a cryptocurrency from scratch." Ongoing, one thing at a time. He had to figure out: how does a wallet work? How does encryption work? How can we send a transaction? How to interact through terminal with a CLI? He built it in Rust to learn the language and aim at protocol engineering roles. What happened: a company reached out wanting a Rust developer — not crypto/blockchain. But he could show language knowledge and something more complex than an afternoon project. It likely helped him get the role — the last senior engineering role he had. He got the job offer within about a week of starting to build it, dropped the project, and moved on. "It was a very valuable journey."

> **Tip:** What actually happened: depth on one meaningfully hard project outweighed a portfolio of shallow finished ones.

> **Note:** What he'd change: he'd do that much sooner. But he'd pick something with a medium level of difficulty that challenges you every day and helps you discover all the other things you have to concern yourself with when building software.

> **Takeaway:** One ongoing, medium-hard project teaches you more than twenty abandoned toys.

### 15. What building your own software actually gives you

> "AI can build out basic CRUD apps … but what it has a hard time doing is learning how to piece all of that stuff together to build a valuable product."

Real, provable experience: the "provable" part is less true now AI generates apps — but even if using agents, you directing agents helps build stronger product mindset.

Product mindset — you start asking:

- What features do we actually want?
- What features do we actually need?
- What is the cost of implementing these features?
- What things are people actually going to pay for?

> **Tip:** You start thinking about these things when you're building your own software versus just learning how to code. You're now learning how to use that code for practical stuff. That's what companies will care about more.

The things AI can't learn for you:

- Real data: GDPR and legal concerns, where data is located for latency, live migrations, protecting production data while adapting structure.
- Customers: actual people with actual needs and complaints.
- Marketing, sales, customer support: all super valuable — becoming a larger part of software engineering.
- Security importance: learned firsthand, not as a book chapter.

> **Warning:** If your examples all run from your local computer, that introduces a high level of risk to a company — especially if you're ever going to touch production data. You might not be getting opportunities not because you can't code, but because you don't know how to code in the context of what the company is trying to do: building production software.

Live migrations especially: you create a design, then morph it into another design for a software change — which happens all the time. How do you do that with data already in the database? How do you avoid corrupting data? How do you make sure the end user doesn't notice anything changed, while still pushing updates?

> **Tip:** "What I know now is that is 100 times more valuable than being able to build out prototypes on your local computer."

> **Takeaway:** Being challenged, and hopefully motivated — that's the real return. Everything else is a byproduct.

### 16. Ship it like a real company

> "I would immediately try to think of some projects I could do that I would push live and treat like a real thing."

If he started again right now, this is what he'd do immediately:

1. Think of projects you can push live.
2. Treat them like a real thing — not a subdomain of some random hosting company.
3. Get a domain.
4. Get branding.
5. Try to get some customers.
6. Do everything a real company would do.

And if getting into a specific area — mobile app development, for example: build an app, deploy it on the app store, get reviews, get users, figure out how to monetize it, figure out advertising, marketing, sales. All super valuable.

> **Note:** And if you're getting into a specific area — mobile app development, for example: build an app, deploy it on the app store, get reviews, get users, figure out how to monetize it, figure out advertising, marketing, sales. All of these skills are super valuable.

> **Insight:** The shift he's pointing at: as time goes on, that's becoming a larger part of software engineering. Can you do more than just write basic code? Can you create features that are actually meaningful for a bigger picture?

And this loops back to people: building on your own can be a lot. But if you're in a group doing the same thing, it's more motivating. Another reason to find experienced people and get into forums and groups.

> **Takeaway:** A live product with a domain, branding, and one real customer teaches you more than a perfect local prototype ever will.

## Part V — Career and life strategy

### 17. Don't marry a title or a company

> "Open your mind a little bit about the company and the job title."

Around six years ago there was a big spike of hiring at FAANG companies, and everybody tried to get into Google, Amazon, Apple. Implication: if you didn't, you were a pathetic failure who didn't deserve software engineering.

> **Warning:** The reality: there are thousands and thousands — if not millions — of companies you can apply your skills to and potentially make a lot of money doing so. And there are so many more roles out there than just "software engineer."

What he actually did:

- Developer relations — interfacing with companies and customers technically, helping integrate software.
- Leading technical workshops.
- Marketing around technical products.
- Pure software engineering, at times.
- Technical videos — created from his basement. The offer was around $240k, roughly $180k base.

> **Tip:** Those kinds of opportunities are equally as good, if not better, than some of the software engineering opportunities out there.

> **Insight:** Where this is heading: pure software engineering roles are becoming less common, and there's going to be a lot of new stuff around AI engineering or variations of software engineering roles.

#### What to do

- Loosen grip on any single title or company. Adjacent roles (dev rel, technical content, solutions) often pay better with better fit.
- Weigh a degree on actual return, build real-world skill in parallel either way.

> **Takeaway:** Your personal strengths may not point at the title you've decided you need. Follow the strengths.

### 18. Break out of your network — and expand your comfort zone

> "You don't know what you don't know."

Meet new people, ideally beyond where you are and where you want to be. Expose yourself to people doing what you want to do, so that becomes your new normal.

Story — the co-founder salary shock: he was a co-founder with engineers from very high-paying, successful roles. When salary came up, he thought — "holy crap, I didn't even know it was possible to make these kinds of numbers." That's unheard of in rural Ohio, where his neighbor is a corn field and the best job is the factory. Those conversations helped him push for higher pay, because he knew what was possible and had seen it.

> **Warning:** He'd never have seen it if he hadn't gone outside his network and location. Starting out, he was completely on his own — studying from his room, reading books, watching videos. "I think this was probably the worst thing I could have done."

The real breakthrough: it isn't leaving your comfort zone — it's expanding it, so numbers stop being wild. "I almost undersold myself. In fact, I probably did undersell myself, because they didn't even blink an eye." Today, what he did for \$1,500 then, he wouldn't even consider now — it wouldn't be worth the effort. \$1,500 seems irrationally low. That's because he expanded mindset and network.

Story — the pricing ladder \$500 -> \$1500 -> \$28k: early on he charged \$500 for a service, worked up nerve to try 3x to \$1,500 — feeling real anxiety submitting that number — only for the client to accept instantly without blinking. That happened repeatedly over roughly a month or two, adding up to about \$28,000, a huge sum right out of high school. Repeated exposure expands what counts as comfortable.

#### What to do

- Get around people above you. Their normal becomes your normal — salaries, rates, ambition.
- Practice discomfort deliberately. Ask for the higher number. Collect rejections. Tolerance compounds.
- Do sales, negotiating, pushing back on demands — things he avoided earlier — turned out to be greatest unlocks.

> **Tip:** It isn't leaving your comfort zone — it's expanding it, so the numbers stop being wild.

> **Note:** "The secret is I'm always uncomfortable." Sometimes talking price or money is still uncomfortable — and that's fine.

> **Takeaway:** The fastest way there isn't more work. It's exposing yourself to different people — and letting that change what "normal" means to you.

### 19. Go more human in an AI world

> "In a world where everything is AI, going up to someone and saying, 'Hey, I would love to have an opportunity to do this with you' — these types of human-to-human interactions are now going to be so much more valuable."

A lot of people believe adopting AI means making everything artificial. He argues the opposite: automate highly automatable work, and go opposite direction on human interaction.

Job applications, cover letters, resumes at scale — AI can automate submission and sifting. Everything has become very inhuman-feeling. So you thrive by going heavy on the human portion, standing out in an AI-saturated world.

His own practice: he might use AI for sales or marketing. But for high-value opportunities, he brings his actual human self. Some people he works with one-on-one, some he provides services to — that's not given to AI. He goes above and beyond to give best support possible. That's the differentiator.

> **Tip:** His own practice: he might use AI to help with sales or marketing. But for high-value opportunities, he goes the extra mile to bring his actual human self into those parts of the business and life.

> **Warning:** Don't go so heavy on artificial intelligence that you lose the human component.

And on mindset, generally: certain beliefs cap potential — assuming you're not able to do things creates the box you live in. ## FAQ

The argument is about pairing: fundamentals alongside AI-enhanced learning, from the start — because refusing the tools "until I'm senior" just copies the structure of veterans who are failing to adapt.</details>

<details><summary>What if I genuinely can't find a mentor?</summary>His blunt take: "You probably haven't tried very hard." Concrete moves: engage on LinkedIn and Reddit, join communities of builders, message authors after reading their books (it works on romance novelists, for crying out loud). And the fallback tier is real: engineering blogs and practitioner podcasts deliver applied experience that tutorials and LLMs can't recreate.</details>

<details><summary>Which programming language should I start with?</summary>It genuinely doesn't matter — JavaScript, TypeScript, Python, anything. Pick one, go deep past the point where it stops being new, and let the diff principle handle every future language. Re-learning loops in a second language feels productive and teaches nothing; learning how the languages differ teaches the transferable skill.</details>

<details><summary>So is a computer science degree worthless?</summary>Not necessarily — he holds one and says the decision is yours to weigh. His critique is of the mindset pipeline: "get the degree and jobs will be handed to you" is bogus, because graduates often arrive without real-world value. Whether or not you go, build real products during those four years — that's "way more real-world skill than most people there" will accumulate.</details>

<details><summary>Won't using AI agents make my software worse?</summary>Used correctly, his experience says the opposite: agents improve system design, architecture, bug detection, and security review. The failure mode is vibe coding — letting AI write everything while understanding nothing. The fix isn't abstinence; it's AI-enhanced software engineering, where you direct, verify, and learn from every output.</details>
