# Building a Digital Brain

Nicholas Cole made over $30M writing on the internet. Here he breaks down why AI makes generic writing worthless, and how to build a personal language model — a library of your stories, opinions, and lessons — that gets you customers 24/7 and gives AI something real to work with.

Source: Lenny's Podcast interview transcript. Timestamps below refer to that recording.

## 01 — The core problem: language is open source, so most writing is commodity

Cole's starting point: the English language — any language — is an open-source communication protocol. No one owns the alphabet. No one owns the vast majority of words. No one even owns their own name.

> "No one owns the individual letters of the alphabet. No one owns words, like the vast majority of words. You don't own the word tomato. I don't own the word catastrophe. We also don't own even our names." [00:05:45]

This leads to the central distinction: **commodity content** vs. **personality content** vs. **original content**.

**Commodity content**

Groupings of words that are not attributable to any one individual. "The key to losing weight is eating healthy." Millions have said it. No one is plagiarizing anyone — it's just a commodity sentence.

> "When you identify AI slop, what you're really identifying is commodity content — you're reading something, but it feels the way that it does because it's not attributable to any one individual." [00:07:00]

## 02 — The three types of content: commodity, personality, original — and their leverage

Not all content is created equal. Cole maps three tranches, each with different short-, medium-, and long-term value.

| Commodity | Personality | Original |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not attributable to anyone | Attributable to *you* | Net-new thinking |
| Useful to beginners | Your stories, life facts, perspective | Comes from years of crystallizing a topic |
| Low leverage as you repeat it | Outperforms more credible commodity content | Can be six words |
| Example: "Save your coffee money, buy the S&P 500" | Example: "When I was 17, I was one of the highest-ranked World of Warcraft players in North America" | Example: Pythagoras saying the Earth is round, not flat |

> "Less credible, more personality content typically outperforms more credible commodity content." [00:31:29]

The liberating implication: you don't need radically new original thinking to build an audience. You need your personality. Your life details. How you see the world.

> "The winning card is actually the personality of the person and how much they're willing to let you into their world." [00:32:15]

## 03 — The mechanism: the digital brain / personal language model

The goal is to build a library of your approved language — words, phrases, stories, opinions, lessons — that AI can draw from. The larger the library, the more net-new combinations you can create without the AI inventing things you never said.

> "The larger your library grows, the more ideas and approved language you have, the more net new combinations you can create." [00:16:15]

### The ghostwriting analogy

Cole spent a decade ghostwriting. The job was never to invent new ideas for the client. It was to take **approved language** — things the client had already said in a book, a podcast, an internal meeting — and stitch it into new combinations.

> "The client said it. So why can't I take a sentence from chapter one and a sentence from chapter two and two sentences from chapter three and stitch them all together and create something new?" [00:15:39]

That is exactly what AI should do for you: not write net-new thinking, but repeat and remix what you've already articulated.

**AI repeats. You think.**

> "I don't want the robots writing for me. What I want is the robots repeating me, which is different." [00:20:11]

## 04 — What to write: the unmade data set: how you became you

The highest-leverage writing you can do is the writing that only you can do. Cole calls this the **unmade data set** — the experiences, mistakes, and lessons you've lived but never articulated.

> "You can't expect AI to write a paragraph about what you learned as a kid going through an experience that you've never articulated. AI doesn't know that. That is an unmade data set." [00:36:11]

### What to capture

- Formative experiences
- Lessons learned
- Mistakes made
- How you see the world — which commodity ideas you agree with and disagree with

> "You want to spend your time building the unmade data set. And the way that you do that is by writing about how you became you." [00:36:20]

**Case study: The World of Warcraft sentence**

Cole wrote a Quora answer that went viral. The first sentence was: "When I was 17 years old, I was one of the highest ranked World of Warcraft players in North America."

He then repeated that exact sentence — or a close variation — in hundreds of pieces over the next decade. Once you find a grouping of words that resonates, you let AI repeat it for you.

### Which commodity ideas do you agree with?

Even commodity content has value. The point is not to avoid it, but to take a stance. Two commodity ideas in personal finance: "Skip your morning coffee to build wealth" vs. "Save your coffee money and buy the S&P 500." Both are commodity. The question is: **which one do you agree with?**

> "It's not the ideas themselves that are unique. It's which ones are tied to your identity." [00:24:09]

The more of those micro-decisions you crystallize, the more AI knows: "I'm never going to use that example because that's not how you see the world."

## 05 — Voice: voice is a bundle of data points you must discover

People talk about "voice" as if it's intangible. Cole argues it's a bundle of concrete variables you can identify and intentionally create.

### The components of voice

- **Word choice** — "really cool" vs. "exquisite" vs. "fucking dope"
- **Sentence length and structure** — long, semicolon-heavy paragraphs vs. short, terse sentences
- **Rhythm** — the cadence of your sentences
- **Adjectives** — the specific ones you reach for

> "Voice is something that you intentionally create. It's not something that just naturally happens." [00:55:00]

### The ghostwriting lesson

Cole once sent a piece to a client who replied, furious: "This doesn't sound like me." Cole listened back to the call and realized he'd used the wrong voice — he'd leaned too much into opinion, while the client always referenced studies and facts. He added two sentences: "According to the New York Times" and "According to Harvard Business Review." He changed 1% of the piece. The client was elated.

> "AI will give you an output and people will look at one individual word choice and go, the whole thing is shit. The reality is, if you were proficient, you understood the qualities that made your voice your voice — which most people don't know. All you would have to do is trade that one adjective for a different adjective." [00:56:05]

A piece is roughly 80% commodity groupings of words, with a small subjective layer of adjective swaps and rhythm choices. Voice is the minority component — but it's the one that makes the piece feel like you.

## 06 — The value spectrum: timely vs. timeless

Everything you create sits on a spectrum. Timely content has high short-term value and low long-term value. Timeless content has low short-term value and high long-term value.

| Timely | Timeless |
| --- | --- |
| High short-term value | Low short-term value |
| Falls off a cliff | High long-term value |
| Example: a meme about a foldable phone announced yesterday | Example: an essay on AI that took two weeks to write |
| Can't repost in a month | Referenceable for 10 years |

> "You can't build something that you know is intended to be very timeless content and expect it to get the same short-term distribution as a meme that capitalizes on an Apple announcement in 24 hours." [01:51:22]

The variables multiply. Add "in 2026" to a headline and you change its medium- and long-term value. Add "Substack just launched this new feature" and you date it. Every piece of content has a value profile. Know which one you're creating.

## 07 — Ownership: association is the only real ownership

If language is open source, what does it mean to own an idea? Cole's answer: ownership is really just association.

> "Ownership is really just a different word for association. You associate ideas with certain people." [00:28:22]

### How association is built

1. **Volume** — write about a topic a lot. People associate the topic with you.
2. **Personality** — share your life details, your perspective, how you see the world.
3. **Consistency** — repeat the same stories, the same stances, the same phrases.

> "Just because you wrote it doesn't mean you own it. If you write the sentence, 'I woke up today and made coffee,' that is not attributable to you. That is an open source grouping of words." [00:44:29]

Original thinking is not something you achieve in three days. It comes from crystallizing the way you see the world in a specific topic for a prolonged period. Pythagoras saying the Earth is round is six words — but he only arrived at those six words after years of thinking.

## 08 — Systems: volume, infrastructure, and the company brain

Cole's baseline expectation: five pieces of content per day. That's the infrastructure. The question on top is: where are the ideas coming from? That's the digital brain.

> "If we're not posting five pieces of content per day, I don't believe we're giving it a fair shot." [01:01:52]

### Branded content

Branded accounts can repeat the founder's commodity ideas — the non-personality parts of the digital brain. A brand can't say "When I was 17, I was a top World of Warcraft player," but it can say "Here are the five benefits of writing online."

> "There's no reason why TypeShare can't tweet, 'Here are the five benefits of writing online.' It's a commodity. And it's the same thing I would have said." [00:41:15]

### The company brain

Cole argues the highest-leverage marketing investment a Fortune 500 company could make is hiring one thoughtful writer and giving them one job: think deeply about the industry and write long-form content. That library feeds the entire marketing machine — short-form, video, sales enablement, product.

> "One smart person could build the entire digital brain library of content that their entire marketing machine uses and reswizzles and remixes and posts and chops and cuts and pastes." [01:07:15]

The same logic applies to data acquisitions: you're buying groupings of words, crystallized thinking, that you can leverage like any other data set.

## 09 — Apply it: what to do next

### The checklist

- Start writing. You can't discover your voice without writing.
- Write from the "I" perspective, not the "you should" perspective.
- Capture your formative experiences, mistakes, and lessons — the unmade data set.
- Take a stance on commodity ideas: which ones do you agree with? Which do you reject?
- Build a library of approved language. Store it anywhere AI can access it.
- Use AI to repeat and remix, not to think. Give it your words, not its own.
- Identify your voice variables: word choice, sentence length, rhythm, adjectives.
- Know where each piece sits on the timely–timeless spectrum.
- Ship at least five pieces of content a day. That's the baseline infrastructure.
- Build association through volume, personality, and consistency.

### Self-check

<details>
<summary>Why does AI slop happen if the model is capable?</summary>

Because the model is drawing from the same data set as everyone else. It doesn't know which commodity ideas you agree with, which life details are yours, or how you see the world. It writes commodity content because that's all it has. Your personality content is the unmade data set — the thing the model cannot know until you write it down.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What's the difference between AI repeating and AI writing?</summary>

AI writing is net-new generation from a shared data set. AI repeating is taking your approved language — things you've already said — and recombining them into new permutations. The first produces slop you don't recognize. The second produces content that sounds like you, because it's made of your words.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you discover your voice if you don't know what it is?</summary>

Write. Publish. Watch what resonates. When a grouping of words works — like Cole's World of Warcraft sentence — note it. Repeat it. Over time you'll identify the word choices, sentence rhythms, and adjective preferences that make your writing feel like you. Voice is a bundle of concrete variables, not an intangible quality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does "timely vs. timeless" change about your expectations?</summary>

A meme capitalizing on today's news should not be expected to perform a year from now. A two-week essay should not be expected to spike on day one. If your expectations don't match the content's position on the spectrum, you'll misjudge its value. Know which game you're playing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is association the real ownership?</summary>

Because no one owns the alphabet or the vast majority of word groupings. What you can own is the association in people's minds between a topic and you. That's built through volume, personality, and consistency. When someone hears "keep drinking your coffee" and thinks of Ramit Sethi, that's ownership. It's not legal ownership. It's mental ownership.

</details>
